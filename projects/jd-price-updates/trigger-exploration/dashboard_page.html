How to read this

  • Drift from last trigger (a rolling trigger point): the price at the last trigger is the anchor. The next trigger is the first change that leaves the price 10% or more above or below the anchor, whether in one jump or several small ones. Then the anchor resets.
  • Single 10% jump: only one update of 10% or more counts. Gradual creep (4% + 3% + 4%) never triggers.
  • "Triggered again within 12 months" only counts triggers we have watched for at least 12 months, so recent triggers don't drag it down. Parts with no next trigger yet are left out of the up/down split.
  • Price is JD list price. Our sell price is list plus the Price Matrix markup for its bucket, so it moves with list. Parts under $1 and 753 unit-of-measure jumps (5x or more) are left out.

JD doesn't change prices at random. Most changes land on a handful of wave dates each year: late March, late May or June, and late August. In 2024 the main wave moved from August to June, and 2023 had no broad wave at all.

Median change at each wave

On most parts the Price Matrix passes JD's increases straight through: our cost, our sell price and our margin dollars rise by the same percentage, and margin % holds. Margin slips in two places: parts that cross into a higher Price Matrix bucket get a smaller markup, and parts where JD cuts our discount see cost rise faster than list.

How margin is measured here

  • Margin $ = our sell price minus JD dealer cost, per unit, before and after each price update.
  • Margin $ % change = (new margin $ − old margin $) ÷ old margin $. Counted only where the old margin was above zero.
  • Cost % change = how much JD changed our dealer cost on that update.
  • Margin lagged = margin $ grew at least 1 point less than cost. If margin $ grows at the same rate as cost, margin % is unchanged.
  • Held / down / up = whether a part's margin % (sell − cost ÷ sell) moved by more than half a point at that update. "Parts we sell" = parts with sales in the last 12 months.
  • Yearly margin $ lost = for parts whose margin % went down: what keeping the old margin % would have made at last year's unit sales, minus what the new prices make.
  • Yearly margin $ added = for every part we sell that changed: (new margin $ − old margin $) per unit × last-12-month units, summed. It answers "if we sell the same quantities as last year, how much more gross margin a year do the new prices give us?" It is an estimate at today's volume, not money booked.

What this leaves out

  • It assumes we knew the wave date in advance. JD's calendar has been regular, but we need to confirm whether JD tells dealers about increases ahead of time. The weekly JD Change Report lists changes on their effective date, so it gives no warning.
  • It buys every part with sales. Buying only the parts likely to rise most would do better. That's what a prediction model would pick.
  • No obsolescence, storage space or cash-flow limits are included.

The size of a part's last change says little about its next one. What drives prices is JD's wave calendar, not each part's own history. A useful model would predict which parts rise most at the next wave, rather than when.

Where machine learning could fit

  • The question for a model: for each part, how likely is a 10%+ increase at the next wave? That ranks which parts to buy ahead of a wave.
  • Inputs we already have: product group and commodity code, price band, size and date of the last few changes, time since the last trigger, sales and on-hand from the part master, and how the same part moved at past waves.
  • How we'd test it: train on waves up to 2024, then check whether it would have picked the parts that jumped in June 2025 and June 2026. If it can't beat "buy everything with sales", it isn't worth running.
  • The bigger win may need no model: if JD tells dealers about a wave a few weeks ahead, a buy-ahead list built from that notice and our sales history is close to risk-free.
